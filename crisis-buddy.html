Crisis Buddy Program

No one should have to face a difficult moment alone.

The Crisis Buddy Program is a peer-support initiative of the Schizophrenia Support Network (SSN) that connects people with trained peers who can offer companionship, listening, and practical support during difficult moments, emotional distress, or the early signs of a crisis.

A Crisis Buddy is not a therapist, psychiatrist, or emergency responder. They are a peer who chooses to walk alongside another person with empathy, patience, respect, and hope.

We also recognise that caring for someone during a difficult time can be challenging. A person supporting another needs support, knowledge, and space to care for themselves too. Through regular workshops and training, SSN will help Crisis Buddies learn ways of caring for both themselves and the person they are supporting.

Learn. Connect. Stay Together.

Our training and workshops will gradually introduce Crisis Buddies to approaches that can help us understand and respond to distress differently.

  • Psychological First Aid
  • Morita Therapy
  • Active listening
  • Validation and compassionate communication
  • Understanding psychosis
  • Suicide awareness
  • Trauma-informed care
  • Recovery-oriented approaches
  • Recognising early warning signs
  • Knowing personal limits and boundaries
  • Self-care for helpers
  • Supporting someone without trying to "fix" them

The aim is not to turn peers into therapists. It is to help ordinary people develop the confidence and skills to be with another person during difficult moments without becoming overwhelmed themselves.

How It Works

1. Register

Complete a short registration form to become part of the Crisis Buddy community.

2. Get Connected

SSN will help connect you with another member based on your needs, availability, location, and preferences.

3. Get to Know Each Other

Buddies can meet online or, where appropriate, in person and spend some time getting to know each other before a crisis occurs.

4. Create a Crisis Support Plan

Together, you can identify early warning signs, things that help, things that make things harder, people to contact, and how you would like to be supported.

5. Stay Connected

Buddies can maintain regular check-ins so that support does not begin only when someone is already in crisis.

6. Reach Out When You Need Someone

When early signs of distress or crisis appear, you can reach out to your Crisis Buddy rather than having to face the experience alone.

When Can You Contact a Crisis Buddy?

You may want to reach out when:

  • You feel overwhelmed or unable to cope.
  • Voices have become stronger or more distressing.
  • You are becoming frightened, suspicious, or confused.
  • You feel disconnected from yourself or the world around you.
  • You are having difficulty sleeping and are becoming concerned.
  • You notice your own early warning signs of relapse.
  • You are going through a difficult emotional period.
  • You simply don't want to be alone.

You do not have to wait until things become a crisis to reach out.

Sometimes having someone sit with us, listen, share a meal, take a walk, or simply remain present can make a difficult moment a little easier.

What Can a Crisis Buddy Do?

  • ✓ Listen without judgment
  • ✓ Stay connected by phone, WhatsApp, or video call
  • ✓ Meet in person when appropriate
  • ✓ Spend time with someone who does not want to be alone
  • ✓ Offer companionship and emotional support
  • ✓ Help someone feel less alone
  • ✓ Offer simple grounding and Psychological First Aid
  • ✓ Help identify what the person needs in the moment
  • ✓ Help reconnect someone with family, friends, professionals, or other supports, with their permission
  • ✓ Offer practical support when appropriate
  • ✓ Where an approved Peer Stay arrangement exists, offer a temporary safe space

What a Crisis Buddy Cannot Do

  • ✗ Does not diagnose
  • ✗ Does not provide psychotherapy
  • ✗ Does not give medical or medication advice
  • ✗ Does not force someone to take medication or seek treatment
  • ✗ Does not promise secrecy when someone's safety is at serious risk
  • ✗ Does not manage emergencies alone
  • ✗ Does not replace a psychiatrist, therapist, hospital, or emergency service

If there is an immediate risk of serious harm to yourself or someone else, professional or emergency help should be sought.

Peer Stay

Sometimes, what we need is simply not to be alone.

An important part of the Crisis Buddy Program is the possibility of Peer Stay.

Some trained peer volunteers may choose to offer a temporary space in their home to another member who is going through a difficult period and does not want to be alone.

Where a Peer Stay is possible, a person may be able to stay with another peer for a short period, share their home, have a place to sleep and eat, and receive companionship and practical and emotional support while they find their way through a difficult period.

Peer Stay is based on mutual agreement, dignity, safety, and respect. It is not residential treatment or professional care.

Peer Stay will only be arranged after appropriate screening, additional training, and safety procedures have been established. Both the person offering the space and the person staying must freely agree to the arrangement.

A Morita-informed Approach

Our Peer Stay model will gradually introduce participants to principles from Morita Therapy, a Japanese approach that offers a different way of relating to difficult emotions and experiences.

Some of the principles we will explore include:

  • Accepting emotions rather than constantly trying to eliminate them.
  • Learning to live alongside anxiety and distress rather than fighting them.
  • Recognising that feelings change naturally over time.
  • Returning attention to meaningful and purposeful activity.
  • Allowing a person to be where they are while gently supporting them towards everyday life.
  • Offering companionship without constantly trying to "fix" the person.

The purpose is not to teach a peer to provide Morita Therapy. Rather, we hope to introduce a way of being with someone in distress that is accepting, grounded, practical, and compassionate.

Our Buddy Agreement

Before beginning a Crisis Buddy relationship, both participants agree to:

  • Respect each other's privacy and confidentiality.
  • Be honest about their availability and limitations.
  • Respect personal boundaries.
  • Communicate openly when something is not working.
  • Avoid becoming dependent on one another for all forms of support.
  • Contact the SSN team when additional support or guidance is needed.
  • Put safety before secrecy.
  • Respect each person's autonomy and choices.

Creating Your Crisis Support Plan

A Crisis Support Plan helps you and your buddy understand what support may be useful before a crisis happens.

About Me

  • What should my buddy know about me?
  • What are my usual early warning signs?
  • What helps me when I am distressed?
  • What tends to make things worse?
  • Who are the people I trust?

When I Am Struggling

  • How would I like my buddy to speak with me?
  • What should my buddy avoid saying or doing?
  • What helps me feel grounded?
  • Would I like someone to remind me about my existing treatment or support plan?
  • Who would I want my buddy to contact if I am becoming increasingly unwell?

My Support Network

  • Trusted family member
  • Trusted friend
  • Psychiatrist
  • Therapist
  • Other healthcare professional
  • SSN contact/coordinator

The Crisis Support Plan belongs to the person receiving support. It is a way of helping others understand how to support you in the way that works best for you.

Become a Crisis Buddy

You don't need to have all the answers.

You don't need to be a professional.

You don't need to know exactly what to say.

Sometimes, being willing to stay, listen, and not leave someone alone with their experience can be an important form of support.

If you would like to become a Crisis Buddy, register your interest below. SSN will provide training, workshops, orientation, and ongoing support as the program develops.
