Finding hope in despair

Reflection from the seventh Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

11th July 2026

Our seventh support group meeting brought together four people, each carrying a different story, yet all living with questions that felt deeply familiar to one another. How do we keep going when life no longer looks the way we imagined? How do we find hope when so much has been lost? A new member joined us for the first time and shared their journey with schizophrenia, beginning in adolescence. They spoke about living with fear, being hospitalised, and spending many years trying to find a medication that allowed them to function while also managing difficult side effects such as overwhelming drowsiness. They also spoke about leaving paid work and now volunteering in a library, uncertain about whether they would ever be able to return to full-time employment. Financial worries weighed heavily on them, yet by the end of the meeting they smiled and said something that stayed with all of us: "It's comforting to know there are other people in the same boat." Sometimes that simple recognition changes everything. Another participant described what it had been like to lose the independent life they had built over many years. Returning to live with family after losing a job felt like losing a part of themselves. Days were spent mostly inside one room. Going downstairs meant risking uncomfortable questions about work, marriage, or the future. They spoke about missing the sea. They used to walk along the beach, camera in hand, photographing the world around them. They had once painted extensively, filling sketchbooks with landscapes and ideas, but years had passed since they had picked up a brush. Opportunities still came their way, yet the motivation to begin again felt painfully out of reach. Listening to them, I was reminded that schizophrenia often steals ordinary things long before it steals extraordinary dreams. It interrupts routines, confidence, friendships, work, creativity, and the quiet sense that tomorrow might hold something worth looking forward to. Another member shared how a difficult week at work had left them emotionally exhausted. A few comments from colleagues had stirred old feelings of rejection and self-doubt, making it difficult to sleep or find peace. They reflected on how easily everyday stress can awaken much older wounds, leaving the mind racing long after the day has ended. We also returned to a familiar theme in our group: the harsh inner voices that continue to accompany many people living with schizophrenia. Although each person's experience was different, there was comfort in discovering that these experiences could be spoken aloud without embarrassment or fear. Towards the end of the meeting, I shared something deeply personal. There are days when despair quietly arrives. Days when even simple acts—getting out of bed, brushing my teeth, beginning the day—can feel strangely without meaning. On those days, thoughts of death can appear not because I truly want to leave life behind, but because loneliness and exhaustion have become so overwhelming. Speaking those words aloud in the group felt unexpectedly freeing. No one rushed to reassure me. No one tried to argue me out of my feelings. Instead, they stayed with me. And I realised how different that is from being alone with despair. Sometimes the greatest gift another person can offer is not advice, but presence. As we prepared to end the meeting, people spoke about feeling calmer than when they had arrived. One participant said they felt relieved. Another said they had found hope after a difficult day. Someone else simply felt less alone. That, perhaps, is what community quietly offers. Not the promise that suffering will disappear. Not easy optimism. But the reassurance that despair does not have to be carried in isolation. Hope does not always arrive as certainty. Sometimes it begins with discovering that someone else is willing to sit beside us in the darkness until, together, we notice the first light returning.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
