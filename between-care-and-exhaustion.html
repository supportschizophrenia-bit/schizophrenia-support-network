Between Care and Exhaustion

Reflection from the 24th Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

14 November 2026

Seven of us met today, including two new members who had found out about our group through the Reddit community.

One of the new members shared their experiences of schizophrenia, which had begun during their teenage years but remained undiagnosed for almost fifteen years. It was only after experiencing an episode later in life that they were hospitalised for several months and received treatment. They had gone through so much in their life, carrying many layers of trauma. Their father had physically and emotionally abused both them and their mother, and it had been an extremely difficult childhood. Later, when their father passed away, they were left with mixed feelings: the absence of a caregiver and the loss of whatever protection a parent might have offered, alongside a sense of relief from the abuse.

They had struggled to complete their graduation, and there was so much suffering in their account that it could bring us to tears. The extreme loneliness they had experienced was particularly moving. They repeatedly expressed the feeling that nobody would come to care for them, take care of them or love them. There would be nobody to love them, and therefore they had to learn to do it themselves. We could feel something of the pain they carried in their heart.

They are now motivated and working as a language trainer, interacting with many people. They have a lot to share and often bring their perspectives and experiences into the conversation. They like to take the initiative and be the first to speak. There is a go-getter quality in them. They also try to bring themselves towards positivity and motivation, both for themselves and others. At the same time, they seem to struggle with vulnerability. Whenever they have allowed themselves to become vulnerable, they have experienced exploitation or been taken advantage of. Perhaps this is why they sometimes shut down their emotions and try to remain positive, motivating themselves and others. We could sense the neglect, trauma and isolation they had experienced throughout their life.

They also spoke about how schizophrenia is not the only factor shaping their suffering. Other circumstances play an important role. They come from an economically disadvantaged background, and poverty brings its own forms of psychological suffering. They also belong to a religious minority, and spoke about the additional emotional challenges of living with discrimination, humiliation and marginalisation. These experiences add to the difficulties they already carry. They often feel that they have to fight alone.

They do not want to focus only on medication but are also interested in other ways of coping. They spoke about their connection with nature and their interest in understanding how different people cope with their experiences. Perhaps we can learn about coping mechanisms from one another and find ways to support each other. They also shared their language with another member, and the two briefly connected through a conversation in their mother tongue. It was a lovely moment of connection.

Another new member also joined us for the first time. They shared that they were pursuing a PhD and spoke about their family circumstances. They are an only child, and their father is a deeply emotional and empathetic person who has been their primary source of care and support. Their mother lives with cerebral palsy, and their father looks after both of them. They spoke about how difficult it can be to live with their own struggles while also being aware of their family's circumstances.

They shared that they sometimes experience suicidal thoughts. At times, they feel that they do not want to live and think about ending their life. These thoughts can be chronic, and they sometimes make plans, although their feelings about wanting to live can also change. They also experience significant social anxiety. At present, they are struggling with the demands of their PhD, which feels isolating and laborious. They are uncertain whether they will be able to complete it, find work or teach in the future. They worry about whether they will get a research position at all.

They shared an incident during an examination invigilation assignment when their hands began to tremble and a student laughed at them. Such experiences make them doubt their own abilities and potential. It was painful to hear how much they question themselves.

Another member, who is working at a university, shared her own experience of pursuing a PhD and the difficulties she encountered. She spoke about how she gradually developed skills and resilience and learnt to recognise her own abilities. She travels for almost four hours every day, which can be exhausting, but she has developed routines around travelling, meeting colleagues and managing her work. She has gradually built an independent life and learnt to do things despite the challenges.

Another member shared that they had worked as a professor of political science for fifteen years. They spoke about how emotionally exhausting the work had been. The teaching load, coursework, preparation and constant demands of the profession had been very challenging. It was a reminder that professional life can be demanding even when someone has considerable experience and qualifications.

Another member spoke about her disappointment at not winning a quiz. She had also been expected to sing at an event but felt that she could not sing well. During the quiz, her team had been slow in displaying their placards and taking turns, and they were unable to answer some of the questions. She described how, in such situations, she and others in the team sometimes go blank when they have to take action or perform in front of people. There can be considerable anxiety around having to do something in the moment.

She also spoke about her fear of losing her parents. When she imagines that they might no longer be there, she becomes extremely anxious. She is uncertain about what she would do with her life, where she would go and how she would manage.

Another member shared her experience of living in supported accommodation in the UK, away from her family. She spoke about how she has been managing, making friends and enjoying her life. She goes out, spends time with people and has found ways to engage with her community. She does not feel entirely alone because there are other people around her, including people from the support group. At the same time, she acknowledged that she does experience loneliness in her life. Her experience made us wonder whether it is possible for a person to live independently, even while continuing to experience loneliness.

We began wondering whether supported accommodation could be developed in India as well. We know of some initiatives working in this area, but we are not sure how much autonomy and privacy residents are able to experience, or whether they can really build an independent life of their own. We also discussed whether there are financial benefits or assistance available from the government for people with disabilities, and whether there are meaningful employment opportunities.

One member shared their experience of having a disability certificate and receiving a travel pass. We also wondered whether the employment opportunities available to people with disabilities are often limited to menial work, such as cashier roles, rather than recognising their abilities, education and potential. Sometimes it feels as though society thinks quite little of people with schizophrenia and does not imagine the possibilities of what we can achieve.

This led us to reflect on whether our group itself could become a space where we grow together, develop confidence and begin to live with greater hope. Could this be a place that holds us emotionally during difficult times, where we find support in one another, and gradually grow socially and emotionally within our communities? Perhaps the group can become more than a place where we share our struggles. It can also be a place where we discover our abilities and possibilities.

Another member shared that they had experienced an up-and-down week, although they felt it was a fairly normal week and that they had been relatively steady. They considered this steadiness a blessing. They are also caring for their father, who lives with schizophrenia, and spoke about how difficult it can be to care for a parent while managing one's own life and struggles. They spoke about burnout and the difficulty of setting boundaries. They are gradually learning to recognise their limits and understand that caregiving can bring considerable exhaustion.

Another member shared that this was something they had experienced the previous week. They had returned home and spent a great deal of time listening to their sister. They were so absorbed in listening and caring for her that they did not realise how overwhelmed they were becoming. They continued to listen until they eventually reached a point where they felt completely overwhelmed and lost their sense of emotional balance.

There is sometimes a fear that we might lose our minds: that we might lose our capacity to think, write, remember, read books and engage with the world intellectually. These capacities can feel so precious to us, and the possibility of losing them can be frightening. Yet we try to hold space for such experiences and states. Perhaps we can allow ourselves to go through periods when we do not feel entirely in control of our minds, without immediately turning against ourselves. We can allow ourselves to experience these states, find care and support, and gradually arrive at a deeper understanding of ourselves.

The member who had become overwhelmed soon realised that they needed to care for themselves. They returned home, almost rushing to preserve their own emotional well-being. They have repeatedly shared how difficult it is for them to set boundaries, say no or recognise when they are overstretching themselves. They are so kind and attentive towards others that they can find themselves listening and caring very deeply, sometimes without noticing how much it is costing them.

Another member shared a similar experience of struggling with boundaries in their relationship with their father, who also experiences psychological difficulties that have not been formally addressed. They often find themselves caring for him and eventually becoming burnt out. At times, they need to prioritise spending time alone and temporarily stepping away from the relationship to regain their emotional space. They spoke about how much they appreciate having that time to themselves.

They described a recent experience when they had become overwhelmed by the tension and demands of caring for their father. They felt that they no longer had the emotional space to continue and had become reactive. They decided to take some time away, went to a café, spent time doing their own work and enjoyed a meal and a drink. It was a small moment of taking care of themselves.

When they returned home, they realised that their father had felt abandoned. They tried to reassure him, express their love and tell him how important he was to them. They wanted him to know that he was not going to be left behind and that he had a home and a place to return to. Through this conversation, their relationship was repaired. They felt that taking a little distance and having some time alone had allowed them to return to the relationship with greater vulnerability and affection. It was interesting to see how a temporary separation could actually make room for reconnection.

The member who had spoken about becoming overwhelmed also shared that they wanted to take some time alone for themselves. They are currently recovering from medication withdrawal and trying to live carefully and attend to their well-being. We hope they find the courage and support they need to move towards a healthier and more fulfilling life, gradually and at their own pace. We wish for them to discover their potential and feel more connected with their sense of self. We care deeply for them. They have been there for others and have offered so much care to the group, and we hope that we can also find ways to care for them.

We also spoke about tactile hallucinations and how painful experiences can sometimes return through bodily sensations. One member shared that, following experiences of sexual violence and trauma, they sometimes have flashbacks that leave them feeling deeply tormented. They can experience sensations of being touched, and these experiences can be extremely painful and difficult to understand or manage.

Some of us shared experiences of sexual assault and the ways in which earlier traumatic experiences can sometimes return through hallucinations or bodily sensations. Experiences of feeling that somebody is touching us, hurting us or pushing us can be particularly distressing. For some, these experiences are connected to events that happened during childhood, adolescence or even very early in life. We spoke about how painful experiences can continue to affect us and how important it is to have spaces where we can share these experiences without feeling alone or misunderstood. We need to take care of one another as we navigate such difficult experiences.

Another member shared that they had been having a conflict with their mother. They felt that their mother was intrusive and was pushing them to seek therapy. They had previously had a difficult experience with a therapist who, in their view, had not taken their whole experience seriously. They had spoken about suicide, but felt that the therapist had responded casually and had avoided engaging with the conversation. They also described experiences of speaking about hallucinations and revisiting painful memories, but feeling that the therapist did not encourage or allow them to explore these experiences.

They were disturbed by these encounters, particularly because therapy is also financially expensive and they were unsure how they could manage the costs. They expressed frustration that many therapists working with psychosis or schizophrenia may focus primarily on helping a person remain functional, without necessarily making room for deeper exploration of their past experiences. They felt that their experiences and concerns were not being taken seriously. I could understand why they felt disappointed and frustrated.

Their mother continued to push them towards therapy, and this became a source of conflict between them. They eventually decided to create some distance and reduce the frequency of their conversations. Earlier, they used to speak every day, but now they speak approximately once a week. At the same time, they are able to meet friends, spend time with them and continue engaging with their social life. It seems that creating some distance in the relationship has allowed them to have a little more space for themselves.

We ended the session with a check-out, where everyone shared how they were feeling after the meeting. There were different experiences and reflections. One member was delighted to have found our support group. They had previously imagined such a space as something they had only seen in films, and now they were actually experiencing it. Another member shared that they felt relaxed and supported, and that they had experienced a sense of being cared for.

Someone shared that they enjoy both listening and sharing, and that they are gradually learning to receive as well as give. Another member expressed appreciation for everyone and for the possibility of coming together in this way. They also spoke about how thankful they were for their family and friends.

There was a shared hope that we could continue to be vulnerable with one another, go through difficult times together and gradually move towards growth, positivity and the realisation of our potential. We do not have to be positive all the time, nor do we have to resolve all our struggles before we can belong. Perhaps we can hold one another through the difficult moments and discover possibilities as we go along.

One member spoke about how the group gives them a sense of belonging. It feels like home and family. Sometimes we wonder who is there for us, where we belong and whether there is a space in which we can truly feel at home. For them, this group has become one such space, something that occupies a meaningful place in their lives.

As we ended the meeting, I was left thinking about how much we carry into these gatherings: loneliness, trauma, uncertainty, fear, exhaustion, questions about work and independence, and the longing to be cared for. Yet we also bring our capacities to listen, understand, share, connect and care for one another. We may not always know how to help, and we may not be able to resolve each other's difficulties. But perhaps we can remain present, make room for vulnerability and gradually build a community in which none of us has to carry everything alone.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
