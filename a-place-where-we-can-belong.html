A Place Where We Can Belong

Reflection from the 22nd Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

31 october 2026

Seven of us met today after a week's break, so technically, we were meeting after two weeks. It felt like a homecoming. There was something about being around each other and finding this sense of home. The group is beginning to feel like a family.

One member shared that he had written down three important questions about his life. The first was why he had experienced child abuse. The second was why nobody had intervened when he abused someone else. The third was why nobody had taken him seriously when he was sleeping on the streets in Mumbai. He feels that his life is a joke. Why was he abused in childhood? And then, later, when he was experiencing schizophrenia, he abused someone else, and he carries the belief that he contributed to that person's suffering and illness. He carries so much guilt inside him and wants redemption. He feels rage towards his family, but he cannot name it because he is not fully aware of what he is feeling. He keeps returning to the question of why nobody intervened and ensured that he received proper treatment when he was struggling from childhood onwards. He feels that it is only when he says that he is guilty, that he is a criminal, or that he was responsible for the Delhi gang rape, that somebody finally attends to him and gives him treatment. He remembers struggling immensely in 2006, when he experienced a psychotic episode, and there was no intervention from a psychiatrist or psychologist. He felt so lonely. He seems to live on the edge of these opposing experiences: being abused and being an abuser, living with schizophrenia and fearing that he may have caused suffering in someone else's life. He carries so much within himself. When we gently intervened and asked him what he might be feeling beneath these questions, he said that he was emotionless. Perhaps this could be a starting point for us. Instead of immediately trying to find answers to his questions, perhaps we can stay with this experience of being emotionless. Where have the emotions gone? Why are they not here? What does it mean to feel that all the emotions have disappeared? Perhaps there is something important in staying with this absence and allowing ourselves to wonder about it, without forcing him to feel something or arrive at an answer before he is ready.

He also spoke about experiencing choking in his chest and struggling to breathe. This happens particularly when he is in crowds, where he finds it difficult to feel like himself. He wondered whether this could be connected to his therapy or whether it was simply because of air pollution. Therapy can open up many challenges and painful experiences, and sometimes, as we begin to encounter things that have remained buried or difficult to express, we may also become more aware of what we experience in our bodies. There may be tightness in the chest, a feeling of choking, or a sense that we cannot breathe. We do not necessarily know what these experiences mean for him, but we can remain curious about them. Therapy is not always an easy experience. It can bring us into contact with painful parts of ourselves that we may have spent a long time trying to avoid. I feel that it is important that he continues to attend his regular therapy sessions and is trying to work on himself, even when the process feels difficult. There is something hopeful in his willingness to keep showing up and to continue asking these questions, even when he does not yet have the answers.

Another member shared that her last week had been fine, but she had been experiencing a strange feeling. There was no emotion and no real sense of anything happening. It was simply blankness. There was an absence of feeling, and it was precisely this absence that made the experience feel uncanny to her. She wondered whether the uncanny feeling was a sign that she was going mad, becoming psychotic, or whether it could simply be a positive experience. She was confused and did not know how to make sense of it. What struck me was that there was no loneliness and no fear accompanying this experience. Usually, when the mind becomes quiet, we may find ourselves feeling terribly alone. But here, there was something remarkably absent, and the absence itself seemed to shock her. She did not know how to understand the experience or find language for it. There was a struggle around what this blankness meant and how she could relate to it. Perhaps it is difficult when our familiar ways of experiencing ourselves disappear, even when what remains is not necessarily painful. We may become unsettled by an experience simply because we do not recognise it. We want to know whether it is good or bad, whether it means something is wrong, or whether it is a sign of something else. But perhaps, sometimes, we need time to stay with an experience before we can make sense of it. We may not immediately know what to call it, and perhaps we do not have to decide what it means all at once.

Another member shared that he was feeling fine, although he had recently experienced auditory hallucinations. He began thinking about the voices, and the experience became frightening. He does not want to relate to these voices in any way. He wants to disconnect from them, cut them off, and experience peace without having them present. When the voices and visions become more prominent, he feels that he loses his agency and fears that they might make him do something bad. He remembered a female voice that he experiences, which feels more like an echo and continues to trouble him. He struggles to understand it and does not know what to make of the experience. He wants to distance himself from it as much as possible. I found myself thinking about the layers of dissociation that may be involved in what he describes. There is already a sense of separation from oneself or one's experience, and then there may be another separation in which the voices themselves become something that must be cut off, avoided, and feared. It seems exhausting to live with this degree of separation, where even one's own experiences can feel unfamiliar or threatening. We wondered together whether his life circumstances, including living with and caring for his mother, might also have involved painful experiences that he continues to carry. There may be difficult memories or experiences that have not yet found a place where they can be spoken about and understood. We do not know what these experiences might be, but perhaps there is room for them to emerge slowly, if and when he feels ready.

Another member shared that most of us have experienced trauma in childhood and wondered whether these painful experiences might be connected to the development of schizophrenia. He seemed to be arriving at some acceptance of himself and of who he is, without needing to struggle against every aspect of his experience. He had travelled to Hyderabad for work and was doing well. He was staying in a room of his own and was enjoying the freedom that came with being away from home. He did not want to return to living with his family because he did not like it there and often felt caged. There was something important about hearing that he was managing his work, travelling independently, and living in a space of his own. It seemed to offer him an opportunity to experience himself differently, outside the familiar circumstances in which he often felt restricted. Perhaps freedom is not always about making a dramatic change in our lives. Sometimes, it is about having a room of our own, being able to move through the day independently, and discovering that we can manage things for ourselves. There can be a quiet sense of acceptance in recognising that we can continue to live our lives while carrying our experiences, rather than waiting for everything within us to be resolved.

Another member shared that her medication had been reduced and that she was sleeping more. It seemed possible that she was feeling less numbed and becoming more aware of her experiences, although she was also wondering why she felt so sleepy. We began talking about how many hours we sleep. She shared that she sleeps for around eight hours, while during psychotic episodes, people may sleep for thirteen hours, and at other times, perhaps only six. Another member spoke about how he wakes up early every Sunday so that he can go out and do photography. He ends up sleeping less, even on Sundays, because he wants to make the most of the day. He also experiences nightmares in which he wakes up in the middle of the night and sees himself at the age of sixty-five, completely alone. He imagines himself in a dark room, with nobody around to take care of him or even talk to him. This image seems to carry so much fear about the future. It is not simply the fear of growing older, but the fear of growing older without anyone to love him, care for him, or share his life with him. Another member tried to help him by inviting him to imagine a good place, somewhere he could feel safe and peaceful. This helped for a short while, but perhaps it was difficult for him to remain there because the fears that he carries are so powerful. The reality he struggles with is one of insecurity, loneliness, helplessness, and the fear that there may be nobody in his life to love and care for him. It is difficult to simply replace these fears with an imagined good place. Perhaps the longing beneath the fear is not only for a peaceful image, but for an actual life in which there are people around him, relationships he can depend on, and a sense that he will not have to face everything alone. I found myself thinking again about the community we have been imagining together. What would it mean to grow older in a place where people know us, where we can turn to one another, and where we do not have to live with the fear that nobody will be there when we need someone?

Another member asked me about my trekking experience the previous week. I shared that I had been in the forest and had seen the moon beautifully in the night sky. It was very dark, and sitting in the forest and simply listening to the birds was a beautiful experience. I had shared a tent with a friend, and we spoke in the mornings and at night. Building my own tent had also felt empowering. There was something beautiful about being in nature, being surrounded by the forest, and experiencing a different kind of life for a few days. I was grateful that she had asked me about the experience and given me the opportunity to share it with the group. Sometimes, we spend so much time talking about our difficulties, our fears, and our struggles that we may forget to share the things that make us feel alive. The experience of being in nature, listening to birds, looking at the moon, and sharing a tent with a friend reminded me that there are also moments of beauty and connection in life. I appreciated being able to bring this experience into the group and share something of myself with everyone.

Another member shared that she had had a relatively light week. For the first time, she switched on her video during the session. She felt more comfortable and realised that she could face people and be herself. She had travelled around and had now returned to Gurgaon. She is also supporting her father, who is experiencing severe schizophrenia and has been struggling to recover. This has made her feel frightened because she has been living with schizophrenia herself for eight or nine years. She worries that schizophrenia is genetic and that it will never go away. She fears that her own future might be determined by what she is seeing in her father. She feels very scared and lonely and does not know how she will face the life ahead of her. She is aware of her experiences and also practises meditation. However, when she introspects or turns her attention inward, she sometimes experiences tactile sensations connected to the memory of being taken away or kidnapped and of someone touching her. These sensations frighten her. It feels as though the trauma she experienced is returning to her, and she does not know how to deal with it. She is scared and struggling to understand why these experiences continue to emerge. Another member agreed that meditation can bring us into contact with painful experiences. She shared that when she introspects and enters a deeper state of awareness, difficult memories and feelings can also arise. Perhaps this is one of the complicated aspects of turning inward. We may hope to find peace, clarity, or a sense of connection with ourselves, but we may also encounter memories, sensations, and experiences that frighten us. It can be difficult to know what to do when this happens. I think it is important that we do not assume that every painful experience that emerges during meditation must be confronted alone or that we must continue going deeper when we feel overwhelmed. We may need support, a sense of safety, and the ability to return to the present. Sometimes, caring for ourselves may mean slowing down rather than looking further inward.

As we moved through the session, I felt that we were holding many difficult experiences together. There were questions about abuse, guilt, the absence of emotion, the fear of losing agency, childhood trauma, and the uncertainty of what the future might bring. There were also moments of freedom, the pleasure of photography, the beauty of nature, and the experience of being able to show ourselves to one another. We were not all in the same place emotionally, and we did not necessarily understand each other's experiences completely. Yet we were listening, responding, asking questions, and trying to remain with what was being shared.

It was an intense and powerful session. At the beginning, there was a feeling of coming home after a break. As the conversation unfolded, we encountered some of the most difficult questions people carry about themselves and their lives. We heard about the struggle to understand the past, the fear of becoming unwell, and the loneliness of imagining a future without anyone around. But we also heard about the possibility of freedom, the discovery that we can be ourselves, and the importance of having people who listen to us. I feel that we are beginning to come together as a community, and perhaps this sense of home is becoming more real with each meeting. We are slowly becoming familiar with one another, learning about the different lives we lead, and discovering that we can bring difficult and contradictory experiences into the same space. We do not have to hide our fears or pretend that we have everything figured out. Perhaps coming home is not about finding a place where all our struggles disappear. It is about finding a place where we can bring ourselves, with all that we carry, and still feel that we belong.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
