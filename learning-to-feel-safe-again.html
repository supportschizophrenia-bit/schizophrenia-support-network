Learning to feel Safe Again

Reflection from the sixteenth Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

12 september 2026

Seven of us came together for our support group meeting today, and we were joined by a couple of new people. The meeting moved through many different experiences—art and creativity, uncertainty about diagnosis, anger, loneliness, psychosis, trauma, fear, and the difficult question of how we find our way back into life after experiences that have changed us deeply.

We began with a conversation about art. One member shared how, after going through several episodes of psychosis, they had gradually found something that had been missing from their life for a long time: expression through art. Art had become a way of finding themselves again. They spoke about how they now encourage creativity within their family as well, wanting art to be something that is not only practised but also felt and lived. What struck me was how little they were thinking about psychosis itself. Their attention had moved towards life—towards creating, making, helping, and being present in the things that mattered to them.

For another member, this relationship with creativity felt very different. They spoke about feeling like a fraud and being uncertain about their diagnosis and where they really belonged. Over the years, they had been given different diagnoses, moving from anxiety to borderline personality disorder, then bipolar disorder, and eventually schizoaffective disorder. They remembered being academically brilliant as a child, until a collapse in their academic abilities and a severe panic attack during their school years changed the course of their life. They now struggled to find meaningful work and a sense of direction. What seemed especially painful was the feeling that their creativity and imagination had also disappeared. Medication had helped contain some of the more frightening experiences, but they felt it had also numbed something deeply important within them.

They spoke about having had an imaginative inner world as a child, with invisible friends and visions, while at the same time feeling unseen and isolated. It seemed that people only began to recognise that something was wrong when their academic abilities began to decline. I found myself thinking about how often suffering can remain invisible when a person is still performing well. Sometimes the gifts remain unseen because nobody is looking closely enough, and sometimes the person themselves does not yet know how to find a way to bring those gifts into the world.

We also spoke about anger. One member described being frightened by their own anger and worried that they might hurt someone, even though they had never wanted to harm others during their experiences of psychosis. Instead, they had learned to turn anger inward. When the anger could not find a place to go, it became depression, despair, and thoughts of suicide. Medication became a way of making the rage quieter, but they also felt that this quietness came at a cost—the loss of creativity, imagination, and some of their deeper emotions. I found this tension difficult and important: sometimes the things that protect us from unbearable feelings can also make it harder to feel fully alive.

Another member spoke about living with voices so frequently that they had almost become part of everyday life. They described being encouraged to imagine themselves in a busy public place, even when they were alone, as a way of coping with the voices. But I found myself wondering about the other side of this—if one is always imagining being in a market, a shopping mall, or surrounded by people, where does the experience of privacy begin? Where is the space where one can simply be alone with oneself and one's feelings? We often speak about safety, but perhaps safety also requires a private inner and outer space where we do not have to constantly defend ourselves or remain alert.

A new member then shared something much more painful. They had been living with intense fear, voices and paranoia, spending much of their time isolated in their room and finding it difficult even to bathe because they did not feel safe or private. They were still trying to work, but everyday life had become extremely difficult. They spoke about having reached what felt like a dead end and about having been suicidal for several weeks. What they shared about their earlier experiences of sexual violence and the terror that followed was deeply painful to hear. The memories continued to return through images, sounds and bodily sensations, leaving them frightened of the outside world and unable to trust other people.

They also spoke about how long it had taken for their experiences to be understood as psychological distress. Before receiving psychiatric help, their family had interpreted what was happening through spiritual or supernatural explanations. By the time appropriate help came, a great deal of suffering had already accumulated. I could hear in their words not only the trauma of what had happened to them, but also the loneliness of having had to live with that terror without being properly understood.

What moved me most was when another member began to recognise parts of their own experience in what was being shared. They had also experienced sexual violence in the past and had carried the memory silently for a long time. They were joining the meeting from a place that held many personal memories for them, and the conversation brought some of those memories very close again. They spoke about having once felt that they did not want to live, and about slowly learning to reclaim public space after what had happened to them—first for a short period, and then gradually for longer.

I remembered hearing this story before, and it was striking to see how differently it could be held now. There was still fear, but there was also a sense of movement. I have always felt something powerful in the courage with which this member fought to survive what happened to them, and it was moving to see them now helping someone else feel less alone. Perhaps sometimes our own experiences become a source of connection—not because the pain becomes less painful, but because we can say to another person, I know something about what it feels like to be here.

The conversation brought to mind the way profound trauma can disturb our most basic sense of trust. When the body has experienced violation, it can become difficult to feel safe anywhere. The outside world can begin to feel threatening, and even one's own body can stop feeling like a private place. Psychosis can sometimes intensify this experience, making the boundaries between what is inside and what is outside feel even less certain. I found myself thinking about how important it is, then, to rebuild safety slowly—not by forcing ourselves to forget what happened, but by gradually experiencing moments in which we are safe enough to remain present.

Later, another member shared a very different kind of experience: a small but significant return to ordinary life. After many years of regularly travelling by train, they had stopped doing so. Last week, they took the train again. It may seem like a small thing, but for them it represented movement, autonomy and the possibility of going out into the world again. I found this deeply encouraging. Recovery is sometimes spoken about in terms of large milestones, but perhaps it can also be found in these quiet moments when something that once felt impossible becomes possible again.

Towards the end of the meeting, one member described the session as light and said that they felt able to be real without having to maintain appearances. Another expressed gratitude simply for having been listened to. Someone else said that the meeting had helped them remember their own feelings. And one member reflected with amazement on how much intelligence, sensitivity, empathy and depth they encountered among people living with schizophrenia.

/

I left the meeting thinking about the different ways people find their way back to themselves. For one person it may be through art. For another, through learning how to live with anger without turning it against themselves. For someone else, it may be taking a train again after years of avoiding it, or stepping outside for thirty minutes, or finding a place where they can finally speak about something they have carried in silence.

Perhaps there is no single path back. Maybe healing is not about erasing what has happened to us, or becoming the person we were before. Maybe it is about slowly reclaiming parts of ourselves that became hidden behind fear, trauma, illness and survival.

And perhaps a support group can become one of those places where this becomes possible—not because we have all found the answers, but because for a little while, we do not have to search for them alone.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
