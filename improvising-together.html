Improvising Together

Reflection from the 25th Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

21 November 2026

Seven of us were present today, though one member joined a little late because of some confusion about the time.

One member shared about his week. He had watched *Interstellar*, and there was a scene in the film in which he was able to feel the relationship between a father and daughter. The father tells his daughter to stay and live in the world, and this held particular significance for him. He feels that his own father never told him to stay, which brings up sadness for him. At the same time, I notice that he finds it difficult to connect with his vulnerability. He often speaks cognitively and seems unable to stay with his own painful feelings or with the feelings of others.

He would sometimes tell me that I look like a famous French footballer. It can be challenging to facilitate with him because he often intervenes in conversations and tries to bring in a positive attitude, offering advice such as exercising, getting sunshine, or thinking positively. In this space, however, we also try to sit with our feelings, especially the painful ones that we may not find room to talk about elsewhere. We try to create a space where these feelings can be present without immediately having to move away from them or find a solution.

For him, the experience with his father was not simply that his father never encouraged him to stay in the world. Rather, he experienced so much abuse from his father that he remembers being told to die. In contrast, he feels that it was the universe that was telling him to live and stay in this world. He felt that he had to fight on his own and find a way to survive, without receiving the support and care he needed. He was hospitalised many years ago, and remembers a time when he felt useless and unemployable. Yet he has brought himself to a place in life where he is now married and working as a soft skills trainer.

He comes from a difficult socioeconomic background and feels that money significantly affects the kind of services and care one can access. At the same time, he seems to look down upon certain aspects of this reality and often wants to know from me what the situation is like outside. I tried to share that certain approaches may work for one person but not necessarily for another. Perhaps it is about trying, exploring and making use of the opportunities available to us. However, the conversation can sometimes become more of a cognitive and intellectual debate, rather than an opportunity for him to bring forward something more emotional about his own experience.

Another member shared that she had an okay week, with some good things and some difficult things, though nothing particularly significant. Her medication has been reduced, and she feels positive about this. One of the difficulties, however, is that she finds herself doing so much work that she is unable to take care of herself. She has written two books, one a work of fiction and the other an autobiography, and both are available through a self-publishing platform. She is interested in writing as an art form, and I invited her to consider writing something for the Schizophrenia Support Network.

She also sometimes wonders whether she will find a job or whether she will continue to struggle in life. She seems to be in two minds about whether she wants to work at all. At times, she feels that she does not want to take up a job, while at other times she longs for the structure that work can bring. Yet she also struggles with the demands of working. Even small things can upset her and make her feel worse about herself, sometimes to the extent that she feels unable to continue working. In the past, a family member would sometimes have to come to her workplace to support her or take her home when things became difficult. She also has a singing engagement coming up with a family, where she will be singing *You Are My Sunshine*. She is not quite sure how that will go, and there seems to be some uncertainty and anticipation around it.

One member shared very vulnerably and emotionally about his experiences of having been abused in the past and of having also abused someone else at another point in his life. These are extremely painful memories for him. He does not feel he has the courage to share them fully in the group, nor does he feel able to write about them. They seem too heavy to put into words. He remains stuck with questions about why nobody intervened or came to help him. Why did nobody stop what was happening when he was being abused? And why did nobody intervene when he himself abused someone?

He has a sense that everyone knows about both experiences, that people know he was abused and that he also abused someone. At times, he feels like going to the security authorities and confessing or reporting both experiences. There is a sense of being unable to keep carrying these memories privately, while also feeling uncertain about what might happen if he were to speak about them openly.

He describes life as a joke, wondering how all of this could have happened to him and why nobody did anything, or why nothing good came out of these experiences. At one level, he wants to protest against life. He wants to take revenge on life, to rebel against it, almost as if saying, "Why has life been like this? I will not simply obey you. I will challenge you." There is so much suffering that he feels has been given to him, and he seems to want to resist the life that has unfolded in this way.

At the same time, he also wonders what the point of living is. Does he deserve to live, given that he has struggled so much and has also caused suffering in the lives of others? There is a painful tension between his wish to challenge life and his questioning of whether he has a place in it. He could not attend the previous week's meeting because he had overslept, and he has been struggling considerably with his sleep.

He also spoke about his work and how he is more comfortable communicating with people through calls, emails or online chats rather than meeting them in person. He has been seeing a therapist who sometimes confronts and challenges him, asking questions about his beliefs and how he arrives at certain conclusions. For instance, the therapist questions how he knows that everybody knows about what happened to him and what he himself did. These questions seem to bring him into contact with some of the assumptions he carries.

He shared that he had been abused by someone in a position of service, and that he had himself abused a child within his family. He experiences considerable guilt about what happened and, at the same time, seems to long for some possibility of redemption in his life. He sometimes even finds himself questioning whether these events actually happened or whether he might have hallucinated them, because of the way these memories and experiences have emerged for him. There is a great deal of confusion, pain and uncertainty in how he relates to these events. Listening to him, I was struck by how much he is carrying and how difficult it seems for him to find a way of making sense of his own history, his actions and his place in the world.

Another member shared that her week had been fine, though she was struggling somewhat because her mother had fainted and fallen ill. The family had been getting medical tests done and was waiting to understand more about her mother's health. At the same time, a close friend of hers has been going through a very difficult time because her mother has developed cancer. Her friend had recently returned from a trip abroad and was now taking care of her mother.

The member seemed somewhat reluctant to share today and perhaps was feeling sad and low because of the challenges surrounding her mother's health. It can be a very painful experience to see someone we love become unwell and to live with the uncertainty that comes with it. I also had a sense that she was carrying some of her friend's pain alongside her own. She sometimes reminds me of a sponge that absorbs the emotions of everyone around her. She listens, cares for others and makes space for their experiences. I found myself wishing that she had more time and space today to speak about her own worries regarding her mother, and also about what she was feeling in relation to her friend. While she is often present for others, I wonder how much room she is able to make for herself and her own feelings.

Another member continues to struggle with speaking. He often feels blank, and there are moments when he seems unable to find words or express what is happening inside him. We stayed with this blankness for a while rather than immediately trying to fill it with conversation. Perhaps the blankness itself is an experience worth listening to. It may be connected to an emptiness or to painful feelings that are difficult to approach. I wonder whether, by staying with it gently, we might gradually find a way to understand what is happening for him.

He would like to return to work and has been struggling to find suitable employment. He often appears withdrawn and finds it difficult to speak freely or fluently. He wonders what kinds of jobs are available in the world for people living with schizophrenia. We tried to turn the question around a little and explore what he himself might enjoy doing, what interests him, and what he would like to bring into the world. Rather than beginning only with what opportunities are available to people with schizophrenia, perhaps we can also begin with the person himself, his interests and the possibilities he might want to explore.

One member joined late and shared about her interests in art and language learning. She also spoke about how much she appreciates having a space where painful feelings can be brought into the conversation. She attends exhibitions and musicals and has recently been exploring improvisation as an art form. Another member had something to add to this discussion, reflecting on how improvisation sometimes involves expressing oneself and sometimes holding back or withdrawing.

This led us to think about how each person improvises in their own way. We may express something, pause, withdraw, listen or respond to what someone else brings into the space. And perhaps the group itself can also improvise together. We do not always know where a conversation will go or what someone might bring into it. Yet, as we listen and respond to one another, something begins to take shape between us. Each person brings their own rhythm, and at times we may find ourselves moving together, almost as if we are improvising in harmony.

I was left thinking about how different each person's experience of being in the group can be. Some of us speak through stories and ideas, some through silence or blankness, and some through the feelings that emerge as we listen to one another. There are moments when we try to move towards solutions, and other moments when perhaps what is needed is simply to stay with what is difficult. We may not always find the right words or know what to do with what someone shares. But perhaps there is something meaningful in continuing to make room for these different ways of being present with one another.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
