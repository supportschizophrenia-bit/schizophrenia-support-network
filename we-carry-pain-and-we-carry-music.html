We Carry Pain, and We Carry Music

Reflection from the 23rd Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

7 November 2026

Eight of us were here today. One member shared that they had been struggling with anxiety because they were participating in a quiz at their church the following day. They did not know what would happen or how they would manage it. There would be several teams, with a number of people in each team, and they were anxious about being there with so many others. Another member suggested that they could tell someone in the group beforehand that they were feeling anxious, so that they might have someone to turn to and feel grounded. Anxiety can be overwhelming, almost like an upward movement where we feel lifted into the air and lose our sense of being grounded. They feared going blank and not knowing what to say. We spoke about how this can happen to us when we are on stage: even when we are prepared, we can suddenly forget everything. It causes so much anxiety and leaves us feeling troubled. Going blank is a common experience, and many of us struggle with it. It is almost as if we freeze or lose our minds for a moment. We fear being judged, mocked, or thought of as unintelligent. We worry about what others might think of us.

Another member shared that they had had a relatively good week and were feeling much more stable and collected. They had been learning music over the past six months, and this had been helping them feel calmer. They play an instrument called the handpan, and they played it for us during the meeting. For many of us, this was the highlight of the session. The music sounded beautiful, calm, and collected. It was wonderful that they could share their music and their gift with everyone. I felt that this was such a beautiful moment, and I could see how much they had become empowered and how they were finding ways to take care of themselves. They were not only learning something for themselves but also sharing something meaningful with others.

One member joined us after a long time. They had recently moved to a different supported accommodation in a remote area because their previous housing arrangement had closed. They had wanted to move into a flat of their own, but it was too expensive, and they could not afford it. They shared that one of their friends had recently become unwell and had needed hospitalisation, and that they might never be able to see two of their friends again. They still had a couple of friends they could meet, but loneliness continued to be a difficult experience, and it did not seem to go away. They had worked for many years as a political scientist, teaching and writing books, but felt that they could no longer do that work. There was now a gap in their professional life, and although they were trying to find employment, they were not getting opportunities. They felt that workplaces in Western countries had become stricter and that people with schizophrenia were not easily given jobs.

They also referred to a WHO report and raised the question of whether outcomes for people with schizophrenia might sometimes be better in countries such as India than in Western countries. We discussed how different social and cultural arrangements shape the experience of illness and recovery. In some Western societies, where there is greater emphasis on individual independence, a person who becomes unwell may have fewer people around to care for them and may end up relying more heavily on hospitalisation and psychiatric services. In countries such as India, and in other more collective societies, families may remain involved and people may have the opportunity to recover within their homes and communities, even though this does not mean that families are always supportive or that people do not suffer. We wondered whether the availability of family and community relationships can make a difference to how people experience schizophrenia and find their way through difficult periods.

We also welcomed a new member who joined us for the first time. They had previously worked as a dentist and had pursued an MBA, but had left their work because of difficult circumstances. They were struggling with schizophrenia and were looking for a community. They wanted to know whether we also had a WhatsApp community or whether our meetings happened only on Zoom. They were curious about where everyone lived and came from. One of their major questions was whether people with schizophrenia could return to work or even attempt to find employment again. They had worked before and were now looking for a job, and they really wanted to work.

We spoke about how several people in our group were working. Some members were employed, another had recently found a job after struggling for a long time, and someone else was also looking for work. One member shared that they worked in customer care and felt more comfortable handling communication through emails and chats rather than face-to-face interactions. They were able to do that work well, and it offered them a way to participate in employment that felt more manageable. They also shared that they had recently received a call about a job interview but had not answered it, even though they had known about the interview. They did not understand why they had done that and wondered whether it made them a bad person.

They also spoke about their anger and the feeling that life had been unfair to them. They had experienced abuse when they were young and had gone through a long period of suffering before receiving psychiatric support. They did not want to have anything to do with their family. They often felt depressed and did not know what to do with the pain. They were feeling hopeless and did not want to be pushed towards positivity or goodness. They wanted to remain with their painful feelings. They also shared that they struggled with their relationship with professionals and felt anger towards them. Their therapist had suggested that some professionals could be good and others not, but they were still unsure about how to make sense of these experiences. They felt dejected and deeply hurt.

Another member shared how they too had experienced inseparable loneliness and anxiety, and how difficult it could be to manage the feeling of being alone. They spoke about watching a film based on the Ramayana and how it brought up existential questions about human life. They were moved by the tragedy of Rama's life and the suffering he had to endure: the death of his father, the abduction of his wife, and the enormous pain that followed. They wondered how he would survive and face life after such losses. We felt sorrow and despair for him, and began thinking about the larger questions the story raises. Is our life already destined to unfold in a particular way, or do we have some freedom to shape it? Is there free will, or are we living through something that has already been determined? It was interesting how a film could open up such questions about suffering, destiny, and the possibilities of human life.

Another member shared that they had started doing something good for themselves. They had begun walking every morning and evening for thirty minutes. Everyone appreciated this and wished them well. They seemed happy to share this small but meaningful change, and it was lovely to see the group respond with so much warmth and encouragement.

One member was really struggling today. They shared how difficult Diwali and the many social gatherings around them had been. They were experiencing anxiety and a lack of acceptance around who they were, particularly in relation to their gender identity. Even when they tried to be themselves, they often felt dismissed as eccentric. They described attending a wedding earlier in the week and drinking alcohol to dull their anxiety and tolerate the overstimulation of being around so many people. They recognised that this was not a healthy way of coping, even if it had helped them get through the event at the time.

They had tried explaining to people what it meant to be transgender and how important it was to use the right pronouns, but felt that people did not listen or make an effort. Even within their family, despite several counselling sessions, they continued to experience difficulty in being recognised and addressed in the way they wanted. They wondered how they could expect others to understand when their own family struggled to do so.

They felt that leaving their current surroundings was becoming increasingly important. It had always been something they wanted to do, but now it seemed like the only way they could live and breathe more freely. At the same time, depression was making it difficult to move towards that goal. They shared that therapy and medication had been helpful, particularly in managing symptoms and helping them stay safe, but they also felt that some of their difficulties were connected to the circumstances in which they were living. Their doctors were aware of this too. They felt that medication could help them manage the symptoms, but that the larger problem needed to be addressed in their everyday life and relationships, not only through changes in their neurochemistry. They continued to move forward, even though it was difficult and they often found themselves crying. There was a sense of having to keep going because they could not see another way at present.

We stayed with their struggle around their queer identity and spoke about films that could help us understand queer experiences and relationships. Another member expressed strong support for queer communities and showed affection and warmth towards them. It was beautiful to see this acceptance being expressed so openly within the group.

Another member shared that they had been feeling better in a somewhat unexpected way. They had not taken their medication for the past two weeks and felt that they were experiencing fewer side effects and becoming more confident. They had cried a lot during this period and could feel their emotions more intensely. There was something about this vulnerability that made them feel at home within themselves. Their sister had also noticed a change and felt that they seemed like a different person. They were feeling less anxious and more collected than before. They described the experience as almost miraculous and felt less frightened of the possibility of psychosis returning. They also felt reassured that they had a support system, including this group, to turn to if they faced difficulties again.

They shared that work continued to be emotionally challenging, and they were trying to take things slowly, moment by moment, and allow themselves time to grow. They did not feel ashamed or embarrassed about the possibility of symptoms returning, and felt that they would find a way through if that happened. It was important to hear how much confidence and acceptance they were beginning to experience. At the same time, we recognised that medication experiences and decisions are different for everyone, and that changes in treatment need to be approached carefully with appropriate medical support. Each person has their own history, circumstances, and journey, and what one person experiences cannot necessarily be applied to another.

I was also thinking about how numbness and emotional disconnection can sometimes make us feel that we are no longer affected by painful experiences, even when the memories remain. There is so much that each of us carries, and perhaps feeling more connected to our emotions can also bring us closer to parts of ourselves that we have not been able to experience fully.

Towards the end, the member who had been anxious about the quiz shared that they were feeling better, blessed, and grateful. They were less anxious about the following day's event and felt more prepared. We began closing our session with the handpan music, and it felt calming and almost like a blessing to our ears.

I felt that this was such a beautiful meeting. In this community, we can sing and dance, and we can be ourselves. We can share freely who we are, without having to hide or explain everything. We can walk our own paths and journeys, sit with our loneliness, and take small steps towards uncertain and unfamiliar challenges. We can make our choices and find our own ways of living. And we can support each other through good times and difficult times. There is something deeply meaningful about being able to come together in this way, to share our struggles and our gifts, and to find a place where we can belong.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
