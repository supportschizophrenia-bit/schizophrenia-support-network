Looking for a Place to Belong

Reflection from the First Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

30 May 2026

Three strangers came together to speak openly about schizophrenia, family, loneliness, and the longing to be understood. As the conversation unfolded, one quiet truth emerged: perhaps healing begins when we discover we no longer have to carry our experiences alone.

Our first support group meeting brought three of us together. As with every beginning, there was a quiet uncertainty in the room. We were strangers, each carrying our own history with schizophrenia, wondering what it might be like to speak openly about experiences that are so often hidden. One member began by sharing how a recent podcast about psychosis had stayed with them long after it ended. It had stirred something within them, reminding them how powerful it can be to hear another person's story. Sometimes simply knowing that someone else has walked a similar path can make us feel a little less alone. As the conversation unfolded, another member spoke about a painful chapter of their family life. They described years of misunderstanding, fear, and difficult decisions made during a loved one's experience of psychosis. Those decisions had led to relationships breaking down, and over time they found themselves living alone, cut off from the people they once considered family. As I listened, I found myself wondering how easily schizophrenia can reshape relationships. Sometimes people are no longer seen as mothers, fathers, daughters, husbands, or wives. Instead, they begin to be seen only through the lens of illness, symptoms, or diagnosis. Something deeply human is lost when that happens. Our conversation gradually turned towards the difficult question of consent. When someone is experiencing psychosis, what does it mean to care for them? When does protection become control? When is involuntary treatment necessary, and when might it cause further harm? There were no simple answers. We sat together with the uncertainty rather than trying to resolve it too quickly. One thought stayed with me throughout the meeting: if we truly want to remain in relationship with someone who is experiencing psychosis, perhaps we cannot always remain safely outside their world. At times, we may have to step closer, to listen before we judge, to understand before we act. That does not mean abandoning our own grounding, but it may require a willingness to enter experiences that feel confusing, frightening, or unfamiliar. We also reflected on how loneliness accompanies so many experiences of schizophrenia. One participant wondered how their own family had lived through those difficult years, recognising that everyone carries wounds, even if they are rarely spoken about. Another spoke of longing for a place where schizophrenia could be discussed openly, without shame or secrecy. By the end of the meeting, what stayed with me was not a debate about treatment or diagnosis. It was the quiet longing for belonging. Many people living with schizophrenia lose more than their mental health. They lose friendships. They lose trust. Sometimes they lose family. And yet, beneath all of that remains a deeply human desire—to be understood, to be listened to, and to find a place where one no longer has to explain or defend one's existence. Perhaps that is what this support group began to offer. Not solutions, not certainty, but the possibility that none of us has to carry our experiences alone. Sometimes healing begins with discovering that, even after years of isolation, there are still people willing to sit beside us and listen.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
