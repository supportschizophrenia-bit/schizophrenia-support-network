When we stay with each other

Reflection from the 17th Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

19 September 2026

Nine of us came together today, and it felt like a very intense meeting, with many people sharing deeply painful experiences from their lives. There was a strong presence of trauma, voices, fear, anger, loneliness and the struggle to feel safe. But what stayed with me most was not only the amount of suffering that was shared, but the way the group slowly gathered around one another. There was something deeply human about how people who were themselves struggling could still find the capacity to listen, understand, care and remain present for someone else.

One member shared that they had watched our podcast conversation with another member and found it powerful and healing. It brought them closer to memories from childhood, including an early relationship with a family member where something felt deeply wrong. I wished there had been more space for them to speak about what had surfaced, because sometimes these memories come quietly and need time before they can really be spoken. Another member shared about experiencing a sound-healing bath, which they found deeply calming. They had been told that such experiences were not recommended for people who had experienced psychosis, but they still found the experience helpful because otherwise they felt surrounded by voices almost all the time. They described hundreds of voices appearing even during ordinary activities like brushing their teeth or bathing. Sometimes they speak back to the voices, sometimes they tell them to stay away, and sometimes they use the radio to create enough sound to distract themselves from the voices because the experience becomes unbearable. They are slowly learning what helps them calm their mind and what gives them a little space between the voices. Someone had suggested that they try to expand those moments of clarity—the quiet, blank space between one voice and another. I found this image very moving: perhaps sometimes healing is not about making everything disappear immediately, but about slowly discovering and expanding the spaces in which we can experience some peace.

Another member shared that they had been doing somewhat better than the previous week and had started writing and journaling again. When they feel overwhelmed, writing gives them somewhere to put their thoughts and feelings. But even this simple act had once been difficult because they were afraid that someone might read their notebook. They began writing on their phone with additional protection, and now they have slowly returned to writing in a physical notebook. There was something important in this small change—a growing sense of trust in themselves and a little less fear of the world. They also spoke again about the traumatic experiences they had gone through, including sexual violence in both childhood and more recently. Listening to this, I was struck by how trauma can make even something as private and ordinary as writing feel unsafe. When we have experienced repeated violations of our boundaries, privacy itself can become something we have to relearn.

Another member then shared about childhood sexual abuse by an older sibling. They had been very young, and the experience remained unspoken and unaddressed for many years. There was a complicated mixture of anger and confusion because the older sibling had also been young and had later explained that they did not understand what they were doing. But this did not erase the impact of what had happened. The member also spoke about their anxiety during school years and how nobody believed them because they were academically successful. Their mother thought they were pretending or making things up. Only recently had they confronted her and asked why she had treated them that way. There was so much anger in their words, and also a question of where that anger can actually go. When anger has been held inside for years, it can become difficult to know how to express it without feeling destructive. I could hear how much this person wanted to release something that had been trapped inside for a very long time.

A new member joined us and spoke about their own experience of childhood abuse and living with schizophrenia. They had been diagnosed when they were younger, but for a long time nobody really understood what was happening to them or tried to care for them in the way they needed. They were now working night shifts and living independently in a hostel. They expressed a strong wish to remain away from their family and not depend on anyone for care. They preferred being alone. There was something understandable in this desire for distance after growing up with so much abuse. Sometimes independence is not simply about wanting to be alone; it can also be a way of protecting oneself from relationships that have previously felt unsafe.

Another member shared that they had been reading about trauma and had almost finished a book on the subject. They reflected on how many people experience abuse and molestation and how much trauma remains hidden. They spoke for a while and then suddenly became quiet and seemed to lose their thread. I noticed how different people in the group respond to difficult material in different ways. Sometimes we speak continuously, sometimes we become quiet, sometimes we need to leave the conversation for a while. There is no single way of staying with painful experiences.

Then one member began speaking about being suicidal and struggling intensely with voices that had become loud, threatening and frightening. The voices seemed to chase her and sometimes brought her close to a catatonic state. She described clinging to her mother and siblings for safety, while also becoming angry with them because there was so much anger inside that needed somewhere to go. Afterwards, she would feel guilty for becoming angry and would become even more lonely. As I listened to her, I could feel how much terror and helplessness was present in the room.

I tried to listen closely and respond with warmth and affection, rather than becoming frightened by the intensity of what she was sharing. Slowly, I noticed that this began to be taken up by the group as well. People started moving towards her emotionally. One member shared what helps them when voices become overwhelming—speaking back to them, fighting against them, finding moments of calm and trying to create some distance. Another member said that they really wanted to care for her and make sure she knew that somebody was there. She also shared that drawing and doodling sometimes helped her. What moved me was that nobody had a perfect solution. They simply brought pieces of their own experience and placed them beside hers.

I had been worried that the intensity of this sharing might overwhelm the group. I wondered whether people might leave because it was too much to hold. But something quite different happened. I found myself thinking that even if everyone else left, I wanted to bring my full attention to this person in that moment. I wanted to give her my affection, curiosity, attention and warmth. I wanted her to experience that she was not too much for us. As I stayed with her, she almost cried, and I became teary and cried with her. We told her that we cared about her and that we wanted to support her. I felt hopeful that perhaps, slowly, she could find the support she needed.

What was especially beautiful was that others began to do the same. One member asked questions with genuine curiosity, trying to understand her rather than trying to fix her. Another shared that they could recognise something of what she was going through. Someone else spoke about animals and suggested that perhaps her relationship with cats could become a source of companionship. They said that humans often ask questions, judge or expect explanations, whereas animals can simply remain beside us when we are sad. You can sit with a cat while you are sad, without having to explain why you are sad. I found this incredibly tender. It was not a grand therapeutic intervention. It was simply an invitation towards relationship, towards being accompanied rather than being alone.

And gradually it seemed that everyone was contributing something. People shared their own struggles, not to compare suffering but to say, in their own way, *I know something about this place*. The person who was struggling seemed to feel understood. I could see that the group was not trying to make her pain disappear. We were simply staying close enough that she did not have to carry it entirely by herself.

Towards the end, there was a deep sense of listening together. Someone said that they could do it, that everyone could do it. Another person described her as courageous, perhaps one of the most courageous people they had encountered recently. Someone else reflected on how far she herself had come and how different she was from where she had once been. At one point somebody asked her to smile, but she could not. And that was okay. We did not need to make her smile. We could simply remain with her as she was.

I left the meeting feeling deeply grateful. There are moments in these groups when I wonder whether we are doing enough, whether the suffering is too much, whether the intensity will push people away. But today I saw something different. I saw people who are themselves carrying enormous pain making room for someone else's pain. I saw vulnerability being met with vulnerability, fear being met with patience, and distress being met with care.

Perhaps this is one of the most important things a community can offer—not that we can take away each other's suffering, but that we can refuse to leave someone alone inside it. We can stay. We can listen. We can become curious instead of frightened. We can say, *I am here with you.* And sometimes, perhaps, that is where healing begins.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
