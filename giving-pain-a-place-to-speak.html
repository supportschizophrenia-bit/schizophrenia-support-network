Learning to Step Back into Life

Reflection from the Third Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

13 June 2026

Our third support group brought together people from three different parts of the world. Although we came from different cultures and life experiences, we found ourselves returning to many of the same questions: What does recovery really mean? What parts of ourselves have we had to hide? And what becomes possible when we finally find a place where those hidden parts can be spoken about? At the beginning of the meeting, I introduced the purpose of the group: to create a space where people living with schizophrenia could speak freely, with dignity and without shame. It is not simply a place to discuss symptoms or medication, but a place where our whole humanity is welcome. One participant shared how they had built an online community encouraging people to speak openly about schizophrenia and to live confidently with their diagnosis. They described recovery as living without shame, finding freedom, and reclaiming one's life. As we listened together, another question slowly emerged. Can recovery also include our grief, our fear, and the parts of our stories that remain difficult to speak about? The conversation gently moved beneath the surface of diagnosis. We began reflecting on experiences from childhood, memories that still carried uncertainty, and the painful confusion that can arise when a person's deepest experiences have been questioned or dismissed by others. Sometimes the greatest suffering is not only what happened to us, but the loneliness of not knowing whether our own experience will be believed. One participant shared that speaking about schizophrenia had become easier than speaking about the painful experiences that had shaped their life long before the diagnosis. The illness was acknowledged; the deeper wounds remained surrounded by silence. That distinction stayed with me. Sometimes society is more comfortable talking about mental illness than talking about trauma, grief, neglect, or abuse. Yet for many people, those experiences remain inseparable from the way they understand their own lives. Another member reflected on returning home after a long period of hospitalisation. Outwardly, life had resumed. They worked, stayed busy, and rebuilt routines. Yet there remained a quiet feeling that important parts of themselves were still unseen, particularly within their own family. Art, music, and meaningful work had become ways of slowly reclaiming an identity beyond illness. As our conversation unfolded, we also discovered that recovery can look different across cultures. For some, recovery means independence—the freedom to live alone, work, and make one's own choices. For others, it means something more relational: finding the courage to stay with painful emotions, allowing grief to be felt rather than hurried away, and discovering that healing does not require us to deny our suffering. Neither understanding felt complete on its own. Perhaps recovery asks us to hold both: the practical work of rebuilding our lives and the emotional work of gently returning to experiences that have long remained hidden. One moment from the meeting has stayed with me ever since. A participant spoke about a time when they had felt completely alone and overwhelmed by despair. In that darkest moment, it was the quiet presence of their dog that gave them one reason to stay alive. It was a simple story, yet it carried extraordinary tenderness. Sometimes healing does not begin with grand interventions. Sometimes it begins because one living being reminds us that we are still needed. As the conversation deepened, something changed in the room. There was less pressure to appear strong or optimistic. Instead, people spoke with a vulnerability that had rarely found space elsewhere. One participant shared that they had never spoken about these experiences so openly before, nor felt so listened to while doing so. I found myself thinking that perhaps many experiences we call mental illness are also stories that have remained hidden for too long. Stories carried silently, often for years, because they felt too painful, too confusing, or too difficult to share. Healing may not always begin by finding answers. Sometimes it begins by discovering that our story can be spoken aloud without being dismissed, hurried, or explained away. Perhaps that is one of the quiet gifts of community: giving pain a place to speak, and discovering that, when it does, we do not have to carry it alone.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
