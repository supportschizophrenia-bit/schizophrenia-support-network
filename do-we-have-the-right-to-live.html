Do We Have the Right to Live?

Reflection from the 21st Schizophrenia Support Group Meeting

17 october 2026

Six of us were there together, and we began by sharing how we were feeling. One member, who has been working night shifts for the past five years and has therefore been unable to join many sessions, was able to join us today. He feels conflicted about many things. He has experienced abuse, and he has also abused others. As a result, he feels that he does not deserve to live, and the question of whether he deserves to exist continues to trouble him. He feels very alone and isolated. He has been attending regular therapy sessions, where he is beginning to see some hope. He is not comfortable sharing all his memories and needs time, but he wants to share them slowly, at his own pace. He feels disgusted with himself for having done something bad. He has attempted suicide several times. On one occasion, he used a harmful substance, and on another, he tried to end his life in a similar way. He then wondered why he had not received help earlier. He feels that he could have been helped if he had received the support he needed, and perhaps he could have made different choices. Possibly, the abuse he inflicted could have been prevented, just as the abuse he experienced might also have been prevented. He now feels guilty about crimes that happen around him. When the Delhi gang rape happened, he felt that it was his fault. He continues to worry and wonder whether he has the right to exist and whether he should even be alive. He had schizophrenia that remained untreated for a long time. His parents did not believe in the mental health system. They thought they were getting him help and took him to a Vipassana centre, hoping that he would be cured. Later, he was taken to a hospital and received ECT. He had been struggling with such a difficult life for a long time. Over the last few days, he had been feeling unwell, but today he was feeling a little better. He went out to the market, ate gol gappe and chana dal, and enjoyed himself. He does not feel positively towards his parents and feels a great deal of anger towards them for not helping him earlier. He carries such intense guilt about not having received help that, at one point, he wrote to a medical institution and also visited one to complain about his parents and the fact that he had not received the care he needed. We stayed with the question that he brought to the group: do we have the right to live? What does it mean to feel that we do not deserve to exist, and how do we live with the guilt we carry?

Another member shared that he was joining us after two weeks. He had been feeling better because he had gone to a concert in Malaysia. He remembered how he used to attend concerts before experiencing psychosis, and he wanted to enjoy that part of his life again. He did enjoy the concert, but the voices were present throughout, and he felt as though he was constantly trying to survive the experience. He cannot simply stop the voices from coming, and we wondered whether it might be possible for him to find a way of being with them rather than having to struggle against them all the time. Today, we also got to know more about his family and the circumstances in which he lives. He has brothers, one of whom also appears to experience delusions and may have undiagnosed schizophrenia. He lives with his elderly mother, who is around seventy, and is deeply invested in caring for her. He does not feel that he receives much support himself. He has to fight for it and stand up for himself, and sometimes he simply does not have the energy to keep doing so. He continues to ask for support because he wants to be loved for who he is. He lives in an apartment complex where many older people live alone, and he wants to imagine a different kind of life for himself. He wants to live in a community, to feel at home, and to have people around him who live together, support each other, and understand one another, rather than simply living alone and suffering in isolation. Yet he does not find this kind of support in his present life. I remembered how, in one of our earlier sessions, we had imagined living together and wondered whether, in the long term, we might create a community where people could live alongside one another. Perhaps this could become possible someday. He feels deeply alone, and sometimes he experiences hallucinations or delusions in which his mother appears as a demon. He tries to brush these experiences aside. At the same time, I wondered whether there might also be difficult feelings in the relationship that deserve space and attention. There may be anger, resentment, or even hatred that he finds difficult to acknowledge, and it is possible that some of these feelings become associated with his mother through the experiences he has. There may also be childhood experiences of neglect or other painful experiences that we do not yet know about. I can only imagine how much he may be suffering. He also spoke about whether schizophrenia is hereditary, as he has noticed that there are people in his family who have been diagnosed or may have an undiagnosed condition. It was a reminder of how questions about illness are not merely biological questions for us. They are also connected to family, care, fear, responsibility, and the uncertainty of what the future might hold.

Another member was feeling lost today. We tried to stay with this feeling of lostness, with the uncertainty of not knowing what we are doing or where we are going. She was struggling with it and did not feel like herself. She shared that she has felt lost from the very beginning and that this feeling has often accompanied her. When she feels lost, she does not always know what to do at first. Slowly, however, she begins to find her steps, make some movement, and discover a few anchors that help her find her way again. She then shared that her week had been fine and uneventful. She had gone to work for only three days. Her husband is coming to stay with her for some time, and she has been busy making arrangements. She feels excited about his arrival, but she is also apprehensive about how things will be. She feels scattered, and perhaps there is nothing wrong with being scattered sometimes. We can be with her as she is, without requiring her to have everything figured out. Perhaps we can be loved as we are, even when we do not feel settled within ourselves. She also remembered her own experience of being kidnapped when another member shared a painful experience. Later, she spoke about the complicated feelings we can have towards our parents. Sometimes we hate them, feel that we want them to die, and remain angry about how they treated us. Then, suddenly, we begin to realise that they are growing older and their health is deteriorating. Before this, we may not have noticed these changes. Their presence and condition may have seemed steady for so long, and then we are confronted with the fact that they are ageing. It is complicated, and the feelings can be contradictory. We may carry anger and resentment while also becoming aware of their vulnerability and mortality. This contradiction baffles her, and she tries to make sense of the different feelings that exist within her relationship with her parents.

Another member shared that he wanted to change his job and move out of his parents' home. He wants freedom and does not want to continue living there. He remembered the time when he lived independently in Gurgaon and at a university. Although he was on his own, he struggled with food in the dining hall and would become frightened that he was going to die. At times, he felt that he wanted to kill himself, and he would avoid food altogether and starve himself. He also remembered his experience of receiving ECT. His family had told him that they were taking him to a Vipassana course, but instead, they took him to the hospital, where he received ECT. He was also given medication mixed with food. He felt manipulated, cheated, and betrayed because these decisions were made without his consent. Now, he sometimes feels that his parents are giving him poisonous food. I noticed a parallel between his earlier experiences and his present fears. The feeling that his parents might poison him or kill him is connected, in some way, to the experience of being taken to the hospital without being told what was going to happen. At the same time, I wondered whether there might be a subtle difference between the experience of being betrayed and the possibility that his parents believed they were helping him. They may have wanted him to get better and to feel better, even though they made decisions for him without his consent and perhaps believed that he was not capable of making those decisions himself. This does not take away from the fear, anger, and betrayal he experienced. It does, however, make the situation more complicated than simply seeing it in one way. He remembered becoming very angry and violent in the psychiatric ward, after which he was given heavy medication that left him feeling numb. He also spoke about a friend who had died and about having nightmares involving him. The two of them had once wanted to visit the Picasso Museum in Barcelona, Spain, but they never made that journey together. He feels guilty because he had given his friend money without knowing that his friend was using alcohol. He finds it difficult to face his friend's parents because he fears that the haunting image of his friend's death will return. Once again, we encountered the ways in which guilt can stay with us, even when we do not fully understand what happened or what responsibility we could reasonably have carried.

Another member shared that she had been in shock after learning that her father had also been diagnosed with schizophrenia. This news had been deeply triggering for her, and she had left home. She was now moving from one friend's home to another. She has a job and can afford to support herself, but she feels deeply sad about how life has unfolded for her. Her father is older and lives in a village, and he does not accept medication or other forms of treatment. Her mother has been giving him medication mixed with food, and a nurse is present in the home to help care for him. She feels troubled by the fact that she has to care for herself while also worrying about her mother and the responsibilities she carries. She left home because she wanted to find a place of her own, somewhere she could feel at home and have some space for herself. At present, however, she does not have a home of her own. She does not want to become a burden on anyone, and she feels scared about what will happen next. She shared that she continues to experience hallucinations, but her friends are accepting of her and sometimes help her when she feels tired or overwhelmed by these experiences. Despite this support, she is frightened that she might become increasingly unwell. There are other members of her family who have experienced schizophrenia, and now, with her father's diagnosis, she is increasingly worried about her own future. She wonders whether she, too, will deteriorate and what her life might become. The group tried to remind her that she is receiving treatment, researching her experiences, and continuing to develop herself. She is actively seeking to understand what is happening to her and to find ways of caring for herself. Perhaps these are important resources that she already has, even while she feels frightened and uncertain. We wanted her to see that her future does not have to be determined by what has happened to other members of her family. There is still room for her own life to unfold differently.

We also spoke about shared psychosis and how two people who are experiencing a detachment from what others recognise as reality may begin to develop a shared reality of their own. There may be a way in which they find an anchor in each other, create a world in which they can exist, and find a sense of connection and support. We wondered what it means to share a reality with another person, and how relationships can become places where we find meaning and belonging. It also made me think about the importance of understanding these experiences without losing sight of the loneliness and the need for connection that may exist within them.

As we came towards the end of the session, we reflected on how we were feeling. One member had felt very heavy at the beginning of the meeting but later experienced a sense of relief and calm. Their body felt more relaxed, as though something had shifted through being able to speak and share with others. Perhaps this is one of the things that can happen when we come together as a community. Another member shared that she felt supported, even though she was still feeling a little lost. At the same time, she felt that there was a remarkable sense of authenticity in the group. People were being honest and speaking about difficult experiences without pretending that everything was fine. She appreciated this deeply. Another member shared that, although they were struggling themselves, meeting other people who were also trying to build a community gave them hope. Someone else felt that they had been able to bring something new to the group today. Talking about family dynamics had felt releasing and liberating, and so had being able to discuss shared psychosis and other experiences that are not always easy to speak about elsewhere.

We will not have a session next week because I will be travelling, and we will resume our meetings the following Saturday. I feel that the group is beginning to take shape. We are becoming more familiar with one another and getting to know each other at a deeper level. There is a growing sense that we can bring our complicated experiences, our difficult family relationships, our guilt, our fears about the future, and our longing for a different life into the same space. We do not have to resolve everything immediately or arrive at the same understanding of what has happened to us. We can listen to one another and allow different experiences to exist alongside each other.

One member said that it had been a wonderful session and that he appreciated everyone in the group and wished everyone the best. There was a genuine feeling of belonging. We had begun with questions about whether we deserve to live, about the guilt we carry, and about the fear that our future might be determined by our past or our family histories. We ended with a sense of connection, with people appreciating one another and recognising what it means to have a place where they can speak openly. Perhaps this is what we are slowly building together: a community in which we do not have to carry everything alone, where we can be honest about our struggles and still be welcomed, and where the question of whether we deserve to live can meet another possibility—that we can live, belong, and find support in one another.

About these reflections

These reflections are written after each weekly Schizophrenia Support Network support group meeting. They are not transcripts, but thoughtful reflections on the themes that emerged during our conversations. Personal details have been removed or changed to protect confidentiality while preserving the humanity of what was shared.
